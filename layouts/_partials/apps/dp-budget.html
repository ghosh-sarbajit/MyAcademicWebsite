What is this for?

The promise. Differential privacy (DP) is a promise to the people in a dataset: anything you publish from it (a count, an average, a chart, a trained model) comes out almost the same whether or not any one person's record is in the data, because a little random noise is added. So nobody can learn much about a single person from what you publish.

The budget. How strong the promise is, is measured by a number called ε (epsilon): smaller is more private. Every result you publish from the same data uses up some of it, and the costs add up, like spending from a budget. Publish too much and the total ε gets so large that the promise means little.

Why no data? The cost depends only on how you will publish (how much noise each result gets and how many results there are), never on the values in the data. That is the point: you work it out before touching the data, the way you price a trip before you book it. People use it to

  • plan a data release (a survey report, a dashboard, a census table) that stays within a budget chosen in advance, say ε ≤ 1;
  • decide how much noise to add, for example when training a model with DP-SGD;
  • check the privacy claim in a paper or a product (“ε = 8 over 1000 training steps”).
ε ≤ 1 strong 1 – 10 moderate, common for trained models > 10 weak

δ (delta) is the small chance that the promise fails outright. Keep it far below one over the number of people, for example 1e-5 for 100,000 people. Methods that add Laplace noise have δ = 0.

Training a model

Training a model privately (DP-SGD) adds Gaussian noise at every training step, so a model trained for many steps is many releases. Enter how much noise each step adds and how many steps there are to get the total ε, or enter the ε you are aiming for to get the noise you need.

The maths

How this is worked out

Basic composition. Steps that are (εi, δi)-DP, each run ki times, are together (Σ kiεi, Σ kiδi)-DP.

Advanced composition (Dwork, Rothblum and Vadhan 2010; Dwork and Roth 2014, Theorem 3.20). For any δ′ > 0 they are (√(2 ln(1/δ′) Σ kiεi²) + Σ kiεi(eεi − 1), Σ kiδi + δ′)-DP.

zCDP (Bun and Steinke 2016). An ε-DP step is ε²/2-zCDP, the ρ's add up, and ρ-zCDP is (ρ + 2√(ρ ln(1/δ′)), δ′)-DP. This needs every step to have δ = 0.

Gaussian, exactly. k Gaussian mechanisms with noise multiplier σ together are μ-Gaussian DP with μ = √k/σ (Dong, Roth and Su 2019), which is (ε, δ)-DP exactly when δ ≥ Φ(−ε/μ + μ/2) − eε Φ(−ε/μ − μ/2) (Balle and Wang 2018). The page solves this for ε, or for σ given a target ε.

Gaussian, by Rényi DP (Mironov 2017). Order α costs kα/(2σ²), and is converted to ε = RDP + ln((α−1)/α) − (ln δ + ln α)/(α − 1) (Balle et al. 2020, as in Opacus), at the best α. It is never below the exact value, but it is what most DP-SGD papers report.
